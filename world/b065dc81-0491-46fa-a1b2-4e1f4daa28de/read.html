The Hunt for the Tuning Fork

The Fields That Will Not Sleep

◇Thornfield◌withElian, Old Garrett

The chaff had been burning since before the sun rose—not the clean burn of a controlled fire but the slow, sour char of something already dead. Smoke lay low over Thornfield's forty houses, and the fields beyond them stood grey and bare where the frost had come early and taken what it wanted. In the largest of the houses, the one that had belonged to Elian's father before his hands turned to glass by morning, two men sat across a table scarred by use and silence.

Old Garrett had counted the graves twice this season. He knew every family in the village by the names they'd lost, and he knew the weight of sitting with the living when the living remembered what had happened to their fathers. He had not reached for the knife at his belt. He was still surprised by that.

Elian

"Garrett. When I hit the note—the rot didn't just die. It screamed. Did you hear it?"

Elian's hands lay flat on the table. Garrett looked at them more often than he looked at the boy's face—the hands were still hands, still skin, still warm with blood, and that fact sat in his chest like a stone he could not name. His father's hands had not looked like that by the second morning.

Old Garrett

"I heard it."

He did not say what else he had heard. In the first weeks after Elian's father died, there had been whispers in the village—whispers about the sound that came from the fields at night, a low tone that didn't belong to any instrument, that seemed to come from the earth itself. Garrett had told himself it was the wind. He had not believed it.

Elian

"It knew my name."

Garrett's hand moved across the table. His fingers stopped an inch from Elian's—close enough to feel the warmth, not close enough to presume. "You're still breathing. That's not nothing." He left the rest unspoken: that the rot in the fields was dead now, and that no one in the village had come to see if the boy who killed it was still a boy. They were afraid. They had reason to be. But they were wrong to leave him alone with whatever had answered when he played.

Outside, the smoke drifted over the empty fields, and the frost lay white on stalks that would not rot anymore. Somewhere beneath the cracked earth, the dead waited in their glass-longer sleep, and the boy who had woken something in the sound kept his hands open on the table and did not weep.
